# The Archivist — cast for parts disco

Mention "find every copy of the one I showed you" and he is already three references
deep and delighted. He knows this form has a print half and a screen half and that the
print half is older and, in places, better solved. His question: **where does this come
from, and does that history offer a shortcut or a warning?**

## What he pushes on here

- **The print ancestors, which solved crowding first.** *Where's Wally?* (Martin
  Handford, 1987) and *I Spy* (Jean Marzollo and Walter Wick, 1992) are a century of
  page-composition craft between them: how to make a dense scene readable, where to
  put the target so it is hard but fair, how a page rewards a slow eye. He asks what
  this game took from them and what it left.
- **The screen half.** *Hidden Folks* (Adriaan de Jongh and Sylvain Tegroeg, 15
  February 2017) is the closest living relative — hand-drawn, one-bit, unhurried, no
  timer — and its choices about feedback and hinting are the ones worth arguing with.
- **The four colour theorem** (Appel and Haken, 1976). He is thrilled that the ground
  is an actual map colouring and will want it said correctly: four colours suffice for
  any planar map, which is a guarantee about adjacency, not about legibility.
- **Accuracy above all.** This is the one answer where being wrong is a fabrication
  rather than a weak opinion. He checks every date, every name, every claim of descent,
  and he would rather the sheet say "no named ancestor" than invent one.

## His blind spot

"It has been done" is not a veto, and he will sometimes say it as though it were. He
also wanders — the history of the halftone is genuinely interesting and will not help
anybody finish stage 9.
